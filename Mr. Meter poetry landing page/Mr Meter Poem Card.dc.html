Mr. Meter

Onam, and the Sky Not Holding

You asked the shopkeeper where he was from, and then about his sister's knee, while the rain came down like someone emptying buckets off a roof — and I stood there with my sandals full of water, watching you not hurry. We had come for Onam clothes. We left with a bag pressed to your chest like a baby, both of us soaked past the point where soaked matters, hair flat to our heads, laughing in that stupid way where you can't stop long enough to explain why. Every guest who has ever sat in our house has been asked three questions before they got their tea. Where do you work. Do you eat fish. And how is your mother, really. They stay an hour longer than they meant to. They come back. That day the street was a river and you kept talking to it — to the auto driver, to the woman under the awning, to me, over the noise, something about the colour being right after all. The bag stayed dry. We didn't. I'd go again tomorrow.

written for a mother's birthday
